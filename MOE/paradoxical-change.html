Principle 1 of 5

Paradoxical Theory of Change

Change happens when we stop enforcing change.

What it is

Arnold Beisser put it simply: change occurs when a person becomes what they are, not when they try to become what they are not. The theory is paradoxical because we do not aim at change directly. We help the young person stand fully in their present experience — including the stuck, the unwanted, the resistant — and from that fuller contact, movement happens on its own. Pushing for change mobilises resistance; allowing what is already there lets it soften and shift.

Why it matters for school counsellors

Schools are change machines: fix this behaviour, raise this grade, settle this child. Counsellors feel real pressure to produce change, and fast. But a student who senses you are trying to change them braces against you — the harder you push, the more they dig in. When you can meet them exactly as they are, the relationship becomes safe enough for them to move.

In the room

A student who refuses to talk. Instead of coaxing, you accept the refusal: "You don't have to say anything. It makes sense you'd be careful in here." Being allowed not to change is often what frees a child to. The same applies to your own frustration in a session — stop fighting it, let yourself notice it, and watch how it loosens.

Five key points

  1. 1 Change comes from acceptance, not effort. Beisser: change occurs when we abandon, for the moment, what we want to become and attempt to be what we are.
  2. 2 The fixing mentality backfires. When each person tries to change the other, the relationship becomes a battlefield of competing change agendas — cycles of blame, resistance, and disappointment.
  3. 3 Drop the change-agent role. This is not passivity but an active stance: supporting people to fully experience and understand their current reality.
  4. 4 Stay with “what is.” Transformation arises from full awareness and acceptance of the present — the actual texture of the conflict and the genuine nature of the difference.
  5. 5 You get the change you need, not the one you wanted. The harder we try to eliminate what troubles us, the more firmly it takes root; staying present lets something unexpected emerge.

Source: Arnold Beisser, “The Paradoxical Theory of Change” (1970).

Try this

Notice where you are pushing — a student, a parent, yourself. For one minute, experiment with fully allowing what is there instead. Watch what moves.
