Principle 2 of 5

Therapeutic Alliance — I and Thou

Meeting the other as a whole person, in the here-and-now.

What it is

Martin Buber described two ways of relating. In I–It, I treat the other as an object — a case, a diagnosis, a problem to manage. In I–Thou, I meet the other as a whole, present being, in this moment. For Buber, "all real living is meeting," and what is healing lives in the genuine encounter between two people. It is not rapport as a technique; it cannot be manufactured. It can only be offered, and allowed to happen.

Why it matters for school counsellors

Young clients are exquisitely sensitive to being treated as a "case". A real, present, mutual alliance is the single strongest predictor of whether counselling helps. For students who have been let down by adults, your willingness to meet them as a Thou — not a file — is itself reparative.

In the room

Putting down the assessment form and simply being with the student. Letting yourself be moved, and letting it show appropriately. Speaking from "I" — "something shifts in me when you say that" — rather than only assessing. And repairing ruptures honestly: "I think I pushed too hard last time."

Five key points

  1. 1 Two ways of relating. Buber distinguished I–It — using, analysing or managing the other — from I–Thou, meeting them as a whole, present being. Healing lives in I–Thou contact.
  2. 2 All real living is meeting. Change emerges in the genuine encounter, not in advice, interpretation, or technique applied to the other.
  3. 3 Healing happens in “the between.” What matters arises in the living space between two people — so attend to that space, not only to the individual in front of you.
  4. 4 Inclusion and confirmation. Feel the other's side of the relationship without losing your own ground, and confirm the person as they are rather than as you wish them to be.
  5. 5 You are a participant, not an observer. You meet through your own presence, sensations and responses; what is needed for healing is already there, waiting to be witnessed rather than fixed.

Source: Martin Buber, I and Thou (1923).

Try this

In your next session, notice the moment you slip into I–It — managing, diagnosing, planning your next move. Gently return to the person in front of you, and name one genuine present-moment response aloud.
