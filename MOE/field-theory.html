Principle 3 of 5

Field Theory

The whole is different from the sum of its parts.

What it is

The idea that a whole is different from — not merely the sum of — its parts comes from Jan Smuts's holism. Kurt Lewin carried it into psychology: behaviour is a function of the person and their environment taken together, as a single dynamic field. The field is the total situation, all of it interrelated and in motion. A child's behaviour is not a fixed property of the child; it is an event in a field of relationships — family, classroom, peers, teachers, you, this moment. Change one part and the whole reorganises. Malcolm Parlett distilled this into five working principles.

Why it matters for school counsellors

School counsellors work at the crossroads of many systems. Field thinking stops you from locating the entire problem inside the child, and reveals leverage you would otherwise miss — a teacher's tone, a seating change, a sibling's illness, even the time of day. You are part of the field too: your own state shapes what becomes possible.

In the room

Mapping who and what surrounds the child before judging the behaviour. A "disruptive" student may be carrying tension that really belongs to the family or the classroom. Reframing a difficult behaviour as a sensible response to its field, then asking where else change could enter.

Five key points

  1. 1 Organisation. The field is an organised whole; meaning comes from the whole context. View the relationship holistically, not as isolated parts.
  2. 2 Contemporaneity. The present field configuration is what matters; the past and future count only insofar as they live in the here-and-now.
  3. 3 Singularity. Every field is unique. Resist formulaic solutions — this constellation of people, timing and circumstance has never existed before.
  4. 4 Changing process. Fields are dynamic, never fixed — continually reorganising as people move, speak, even breathe.
  5. 5 Possible relevance. Every phenomenon — a glance, a sound outside, the furniture, a fleeting thought — is potentially meaningful; nothing is dismissed in advance.

Source: Jan Smuts, Holism and Evolution (1926); Kurt Lewin (field theory); Malcolm Parlett, “Reflections on Field Theory” (1991).

Try this

For any case, sketch the child at the centre and map the field around them — family, teachers, peers, you. Ask: what is this behaviour solving within this field? Where else could I intervene?
